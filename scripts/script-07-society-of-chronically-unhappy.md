## SCRIPT

**Interviewer:** You once said: "We live in a society of chronically unhappy people." How did you arrive at such a bold statement?

**Erich Fromm:** To me, that statement isn't bold at all – quite the opposite. If one only opens their eyes, one can see it. Most people pretend – to themselves as well – that they are happy. Because if you're unhappy, you're considered – as one would say in English – a failure. So you must wear the mask of contentment, of happiness, otherwise you lose credit on the market. Then you're no longer a normal, capable person. But just look at people – it's plain to see what lies behind the mask: Restlessness, irritability, anger, depression, sleeplessness, unhappiness – what the French called "la malaise." Already at the beginning of the century people spoke of the "malaise du siècle" – what Freud called "the discontent in civilization." But it isn't discontent in civilization – it is discontent in bourgeois society, which turns man into a work animal. Everything that truly matters – the ability to love, to be there for oneself and for others, to think, not to be an instrument of the economy but its purpose – that is what makes people the way they are. I believe it's a general fiction people share, that modern man is happy. But this observation is not mine alone – you can find it in the work of many others. One only needs to open one's eyes and not be deceived by appearances.

**Interviewer:** Not be deceived by appearances, as you once said as a therapist – this comes from your experience in therapy, doesn't it?

**Erich Fromm:** Exactly. I've been analyzing since 1926 and supervised hundreds of cases by younger colleagues. These are empirical findings. People came as patients usually because of small symptoms, this or that. What I first had to help them realize was that they were deeply unhappy. That they were dissatisfied with life, that life seemed meaningless – and that from this came their various symptoms, attempts to compensate for their unhappiness.

**Interviewer:** Does that mean that the people we call "normal" are, from your point of view, actually ill?

**Erich Fromm:** Oh yes. The most normal are the sickest, and the sick are the healthiest. That may sound witty or exaggerated, but I mean it quite seriously – it's not a clever phrase. The person who is ill shows that certain human qualities in them are not yet so suppressed that they no longer conflict with cultural patterns. And that conflict – that friction – produces symptoms. A symptom, like pain, is only a sign that something is wrong. Happy is the one who has a symptom, as happy as the one who feels pain when something is missing. We know that if humans couldn't feel pain, they would be in great danger. But many people – the so-called "normal" ones – are so adapted, have so abandoned what is truly their own, are so alienated, so mechanical, so robotic, that they no longer even feel conflict. Their real feelings, their love, their hate – all that has been so repressed or atrophied that they resemble a state of chronic mild schizophrenia.

**Interviewer:** Do you see the causes for this in our society?

**Erich Fromm:** The causes seem quite obvious to me. Our society is built on the principle that the goal of life is ever greater production – as compensation and as necessity – and ever greater consumption. Economic and technological progress – that is what we live for, not the human being. What benefits man matters little. Even what harms him hardly matters. Much of it is well known: advertisements promote things that are deadly and harmful. For example – as was recently discussed – milk shipped to Africa that persuades women not to nurse their children, leading to many infant deaths. But that doesn't matter – when profit is at stake, there's no conscience about harming people. And if you want an even starker example, just think of profits from the arms industry, the sale of weapons across the world, even atomic energy. What happens to people in the process is irrelevant. That began only in the industrial age. In the Middle Ages it wasn't so – nor in most cultures. Back then, culture served humanity, and the economy was a means, not an end. Freud himself offered one of the sharpest critiques of bourgeois society, without realizing it. Bourgeois society is a neurotic one – ruled by the principle of having: of accumulating, hoarding, clinging, of greed in the end.

**Interviewer:** Then, following that logic, Mr. Fromm, would the industrial society actually be the underdeveloped one?

**Erich Fromm:** Humanly speaking, it certainly is the underdeveloped society. If I look at a tribal society in Africa, or a simple peasant community where such life still exists – they are far more developed, humanly, than we are. Modern man believes that the more machines one needs, the more advanced one is. But in truth, the fewer machines one needs, the more developed one is. And so he looks down upon those who do not build, use, or depend on machines.
