## SCRIPT

I want to tell you something that most people will not say to you, and that I have spent my whole life saying anyway: we live in a society of chronically unhappy people. Not occasionally unhappy. Not unhappy for a season, or because of one bad year. Chronically. As a permanent condition, dressed up every single morning to look like something else.

Before I go on, I want to ask you for something. If what I'm about to say stays with you, click "like," subscribe to this channel, and write in the comments the words "I want to see behind the mask" — so I know you're ready to walk this path with me.

Before I explain what I mean by that, let me tell you briefly who is speaking to you. I was born in Frankfurt am Main in the year 1900, into an Orthodox Jewish family, and I spent much of my youth trying to understand something that frightened me: how otherwise reasonable, otherwise decent people could be swept into collective madness. I watched it happen twice — once as a boy, during the First World War, and again as a grown man, when Nazism rose in my own country and forced me to flee in 1933. In between, I trained as a psychoanalyst at the Berlin Institute of Psychoanalysis, beginning in 1926, after undergoing my own analysis first. I tell you this because everything I am about to say to you about "empirical findings" is not armchair philosophy. It is the residue of decades spent in a room with real, suffering people. I was part of the circle of thinkers history now calls the Frankfurt School, where for the first time psychoanalysis was turned, at real scale, into a tool for examining not only the individual mind, but the sickness of entire societies. That is the lens I bring to everything I am about to tell you. Keep it in mind, because you will notice my answers keep sliding from the personal to the political without ever feeling, to me, like a change of subject. For me, there was never a meaningful difference. The patient on my couch and the society outside my window were the same illness, viewed at two different distances.

That refusal to separate the psychological from the economic became the organizing idea of my entire life's work, and I gave it a name: social character. I came to believe that every society, in order to function, needs its members to want to do what they have to do — to feel, from the inside, a genuine desire to work the jobs, buy the goods, and live the life that the economic system requires of them. A society does not survive by force alone. It survives by shaping character itself, from childhood onward, until the demands of the system feel like the natural preferences of the individual. I called that shaping process the social character of a culture — not each person's private, unique personality, but the shared core of drives and values stamped into nearly everyone who grows up inside the same economic structure. It is a quiet, almost invisible kind of engineering. Nobody sits a child down and instructs them to prize productivity over rest, or possessions over relationships. The economy does that instruction for them, continuously, through what gets rewarded and what gets ignored. By the time I am telling you this, late in my life, I am no longer describing a theory to you. I am describing a diagnosis I have watched confirmed, patient after patient, for half a century.

People have often asked me how I arrived at something as bold as saying we live in a society of the chronically unhappy. So let me answer that plainly, the way I always have. To me, that statement isn't bold at all – quite the opposite. If one only opens their eyes, one can see it. Most people pretend – to themselves as well – that they are happy. Because if you're unhappy, you're considered – as one would say in English – a failure. So you must wear the mask of contentment, of happiness, otherwise you lose credit on the market. Then you're no longer a normal, capable person. But just look at people – it's plain to see what lies behind the mask: restlessness, irritability, anger, depression, sleeplessness, unhappiness – what the French called "la malaise." Already at the beginning of this century people spoke of the "malaise du siècle" – what Freud called "the discontent in civilization." But it isn't discontent in civilization – it is discontent in bourgeois society, which turns man into a work animal. Everything that truly matters – the ability to love, to be there for oneself and for others, to think, not to be an instrument of the economy but its purpose – that is what makes people the way they are. I believe it's a general fiction people share, that modern man is happy. But this observation is not mine alone – you can find it in the work of many others. One only needs to open one's eyes and not be deceived by appearances.

Notice the phrase I reach for almost without thinking: "you lose credit on the market." I am not being poetic. I mean it in the most literal, economic sense I can. Years earlier, in my book *Man for Himself*, I had already described what I called the marketing orientation — a way of relating to your own personality as if it were a product to be sold. Under the marketing orientation, a person does not ask "who am I, and what do I feel?" They ask "what does the market want me to be right now, so that I remain in demand?" Success is not measured by whether your inner life is coherent or whole. It is measured by whether you sell — whether you are liked, promoted, approved of, invited back. And the terrifying part of this, which I have spent my career trying to make visible, is that the mask stops feeling like a mask. It becomes the only self the person has left access to. I called the end state of this process automaton conformity: a person who has surrendered so completely to what is expected of them that they no longer experience the surrender as loss. They feel, if anything, relief — because an automaton is never anxious about failing to be itself. It has nothing left to fail at. That is the machinery running underneath my sentence "you must wear the mask of contentment." It is not vanity. It is a survival strategy in a system that prices unhappiness as a defect.

Automaton conformity was never the only exit from that anxiety I described — only the quietest one. In my 1941 book *Escape from Freedom*, the one that first made my name, written in the shadow of the Nazism I had just fled, I laid out three ways a person can respond to the unbearable freedom and isolation of modern life, once the old certainties of tradition, community, and fixed social roles have dissolved. One path is authoritarianism — surrendering the self not to a faceless market this time, but to a strongman, a party, an ideology, fusing your identity with something that feels bigger and more certain than you are, so the terror of standing alone disappears. Another is destructiveness — turning outward, against the world, in an attempt to eliminate the very things that make you feel small and threatened. And the third, the one most relevant to what I'm telling you now, is automaton conformity — the quiet path, the one that doesn't march or rage, but simply dissolves the self into whatever the surrounding culture expects, until there is no longer a private self left to feel afraid. I wrote that book to explain how an educated, cultured nation could hand itself over to Hitler. Today, I am describing the same escape mechanism operating in peacetime democracies, without tanks or rallies — just a mask, worn every morning, because the alternative feels too exposed.

This is also where a distinction I have made throughout my work quietly belongs here — the difference between happiness and mere pleasure. The mask of contentment produces something that looks like happiness from the outside — a person who smiles, performs, and is rewarded for it — but I have insisted throughout my work that this kind of manufactured cheerfulness is not the same thing as a life actually being lived well. Real well-being, to me, can never be reduced to the momentary relief of having pleased someone else, or having sold well on the market that day. It requires something closer to wholeness: a person's feelings, thoughts, and actions all pointing in the same direction, rather than the feelings buried under a performance designed for other people's approval. That gap — between looking content and being whole — is precisely the gap my patients kept falling into, and precisely the gap I mean when I tell you people are restless, irritable, angry, depressed, sleepless, underneath a face arranged to suggest otherwise.

You might also ask me whether this insight comes from an abstract theory, or from something I have watched with my own eyes, again and again, as a therapist. Let me be direct about that too. I've been analyzing since 1926 and supervised hundreds of cases by younger colleagues. These are empirical findings. People came as patients usually because of small symptoms, this or that. What I first had to help them realize was that they were deeply unhappy. That they were dissatisfied with life, that life seemed meaningless – and that from this came their various symptoms, attempts to compensate for their unhappiness.

This is a crucial hinge in what I am telling you, easy to rush past. I am drawing a straight line from Freud's individual unconscious to something Freud himself never fully mapped — what I called the social unconscious: the vast portion of a person's experience that is repressed not because it is personally shameful, but because the surrounding society has no category for it, no language that would let it be spoken and heard. Freud's unconscious was built mostly out of forbidden individual drives, things a single person's own upbringing had taught them to bury. My addition was to ask: what if an entire society, through its language, its rewarded behaviors, and its unspoken assumptions, teaches millions of people at once to bury the same experiences? What if a whole culture, in effect, agrees not to notice something, and each individual inherits that blind spot along with everything else they're handed at birth? A patient arrives with insomnia, or a nagging anxiety, or an irritability they cannot explain, and the presenting complaint is almost never the real subject. Underneath it, there is nearly always the same discovery waiting: this person has quietly concluded that their life is meaningless, and has never once been permitted to say so out loud, not even to themselves, because nothing in the culture around them gave that feeling a name or a place to go. The symptom is what leaks out when meaning has nowhere else to go. By the time I am telling you this, I have spent close to fifty years watching that same pattern repeat, across patients, across countries, across decades — which is why I call it empirical. I am not hypothesizing. I am reporting a result, observed so many times it has stopped being a theory to me and become, simply, a fact about modern life.

That fact leads directly to the most provocative thing I will tell you — the moment I invert the entire premise of what health and illness even mean. You might ask me whether this means the people we call "normal" are, in my view, actually ill. Oh yes. The most normal are the sickest, and the sick are the healthiest. That may sound witty or exaggerated, but I mean it quite seriously – it's not a clever phrase. The person who is ill shows that certain human qualities in them are not yet so suppressed that they no longer conflict with cultural patterns. And that conflict – that friction – produces symptoms. A symptom, like pain, is only a sign that something is wrong. Happy is the one who has a symptom, as happy as the one who feels pain when something is missing. We know that if humans couldn't feel pain, they would be in great danger. But many people – the so-called "normal" ones – are so adapted, have so abandoned what is truly their own, are so alienated, so mechanical, so robotic, that they no longer even feel conflict. Their real feelings, their love, their hate – all that has been so repressed or atrophied that they resemble a state of chronic mild schizophrenia.

This is not a throwaway paradox for me. It is the explicit, load-bearing thesis of my 1955 book *The Sane Society*, where I argue that a society itself can be sick, and that when it is, the people who adapt to it most completely are not the healthy ones — they are the most thoroughly infected. I gave this condition a name that has outlived the book itself: the pathology of normalcy. The idea is unsettling precisely because it removes the usual escape hatch. We are used to thinking of mental illness as a private failure, a deviation from a healthy baseline everyone else occupies. I turn that completely around. If the baseline itself — the culture, the economy, the accepted way of living — is what's diseased, then conforming perfectly to it is not evidence of health. It is evidence of the deepest possible infection, one so complete the person no longer feels the friction that would normally announce that something is wrong. That is what I mean when I say the "normal" person has become mechanical and robotic enough to resemble a state of chronic mild schizophrenia — not a clinical diagnosis, but a description of a self so divided from its own real feelings that it has stopped noticing the split. Meanwhile the patient with a visible symptom — the insomnia, the anxiety, the depression — is, paradoxically, still fighting. Some part of them still refuses to fully surrender to a system I believe was built to devour exactly what is most human in a person. The symptom is the last flicker of resistance. That is why, in my view, it is almost a mark of health.

This is also the point in my thinking where psychology stops being a private matter at all and becomes, unavoidably, a political one. If entire populations can be rendered sick by the structure of the society they live in, then treating the individual patient — helping one person adjust, calm down, cope better — can only ever be a partial answer. A therapist working from that premise cannot, in good conscience, treat successful adjustment to a sick society as the goal of therapy, because perfect adjustment is exactly the condition I have just finished describing as the deepest form of the illness. This is part of why I never comfortably fit the label of a purely clinical psychoanalyst. I kept insisting that the couch and the culture were the same case file, and that no diagnosis of the individual was complete without a diagnosis of the society standing behind them.

So where do I see the causes of all this? Let me tell you plainly, because to me they are quite obvious. Our society is built on the principle that the goal of life is ever greater production – as compensation and as necessity – and ever greater consumption. Economic and technological progress – that is what we live for, not the human being. What benefits man matters little. Even what harms him hardly matters. Much of it is well known: advertisements promote things that are deadly and harmful. For example – as was recently discussed – milk shipped to Africa that persuades women not to nurse their children, leading to many infant deaths. But that doesn't matter – when profit is at stake, there's no conscience about harming people. And if you want an even starker example, just think of profits from the arms industry, the sale of weapons across the world, even atomic energy. What happens to people in the process is irrelevant. That began only in the industrial age. In the Middle Ages it wasn't so – nor in most cultures. Back then, culture served humanity, and the economy was a means, not an end. Freud himself offered one of the sharpest critiques of bourgeois society, without realizing it. Bourgeois society is a neurotic one – ruled by the principle of having: of accumulating, hoarding, clinging, of greed in the end.

Notice, too, the exact words I chose just now — "accumulating, hoarding, clinging." Those are not casual synonyms I reached for in the moment. Decades earlier, in *Man for Himself*, I had already mapped out a typology of what I called character orientations, and one of them I named, precisely, the hoarding orientation: a way of relating to the world built on holding on — to money, to possessions, to feelings, even to other people — for fear that what is not held onto will be lost forever. I set that against what I called the productive orientation, the capacity to relate to the world by creating, loving, and reasoning rather than by accumulating. When I tell you bourgeois society is ruled by the principle of having, I am not coining a new idea on the spot. I am reaching for vocabulary I had already spent thirty years refining, and applying it, in this very moment, to the whole civilization around us.

That phrase — "the principle of having" — is the seed of what became, in 1976, the book many consider the final major statement of my life's work: *To Have or to Be?* My argument there is deceptively simple. There are two fundamentally different ways a person can relate to existence. In the having mode, your sense of self is built out of what you possess — property, status, credentials, relationships treated as trophies, even ideas hoarded rather than lived. The trouble with building an identity this way is that it is permanently unstable, because everything you have can be lost. I put the danger in a single question I have asked audiences for years: if I am what I have, and if I lose what I have, who then am I? In the being mode, by contrast, your sense of self comes from what you actively do and experience — loving, creating, thinking, growing — activity that cannot be confiscated, because it isn't a possession in the first place. I believe industrial capitalism has organized an entire civilization almost exclusively around the having mode, and has gone further still, manufacturing what I call homo consumens — consuming man — a human being whose identity is measured, and constantly re-measured, by what they acquire and use up. That is the economic machine underneath the small, devastating example I gave you of infant formula marketed to mothers in Africa: a system that will manufacture demand and sell a product even when the product costs infant lives, because within the having mode, profit is the only variable the system is actually designed to protect. Not because the individuals running it are monsters — I want to be careful never to say that — but because the logic of the system doesn't include a column for human cost. It was never built to.

Now follow that logic with me one step further, because it leads somewhere most people never expect. If the goal of an industrial society is ever more production and consumption, would that society, then, actually be the underdeveloped one? Humanly speaking, it certainly is the underdeveloped society. If I look at a tribal society in Africa, or a simple peasant community where such life still exists – they are far more developed, humanly, than we are. Modern man believes that the more machines one needs, the more advanced one is. But in truth, the fewer machines one needs, the more developed one is. And so he looks down upon those who do not build, use, or depend on machines.

It is worth sitting with how radical that reversal actually is, because it is easy to nod along and miss what I am doing to the entire vocabulary of "progress." I am not romanticizing poverty, and I am not naive about hardship. What I am rejecting is the assumption — so deeply built into modern life that it rarely gets questioned at all — that technological complexity and human development are the same measurement. Elsewhere in my work I put the stakes of that confusion in blunt terms: the danger of the past was that men became slaves; the danger of the future, I warned, is that men may become robots. A robot, in my sense, is not science fiction. It is a fully adapted, fully "normal," fully productive member of an industrial society who has quietly stopped being able to love, to feel, to want anything that isn't first approved by the market. By that measurement — not output, not GDP, not machines owned — a community that has kept its capacity to love, to grieve openly, to sit with another person without a screen or a schedule between them, is, in my own phrase, more developed. Not more comfortable. Not wealthier. More developed, as a human being.

I gave this tension its fullest and darkest treatment a few years before this reflection, in my 1973 book *The Anatomy of Human Destructiveness*, where I introduced a pair of concepts I consider the deepest fork in human character: biophilia and necrophilia. Biophilia, as I defined it, is the passionate love of life and of everything alive — a person, a plant, an idea, a social group — the drive to help things grow rather than to control or freeze them. Necrophilia, in my specific psychological sense, has nothing to do with the clinical meaning of that word. I used it to describe a passionate attraction to everything dead, mechanical, and fixed — an orientation that would rather dominate and control the living than tolerate its unpredictability, that prefers the certainty of machines to the mess of people. My warning, in that book and in the years since, is that a civilization built entirely around production, machinery, and control is quietly cultivating necrophilia at a civilizational scale — teaching its members, generation after generation, to feel more at ease with the mechanical and the controllable than with the living and the unpredictable. That is the deeper current underneath my claim that a society measuring its worth in machines has the measurement backward. It isn't only inefficient by my standard. It is training its people, slowly and invisibly, to love the wrong things.

Let me draw the thread through everything I have just told you, because it is easy to experience it as five separate answers rather than one argument. I started with a mask — the performed happiness everyone agrees to wear. I traced that mask back to decades of clinical evidence that behind it sits real, undiagnosed despair. I inverted the entire concept of normal and sick, so that adaptation itself becomes suspect. I located the engine of all of it in an economic system organized around having rather than being. And I ended by asking you to consider that the society proudest of its own advancement might, by the only measurement I consider worth taking — how fully alive its people actually are — be the most underdeveloped one on earth.

None of this is despair for its own sake. I have spent my whole life insisting the opposite is possible — that a person can still choose the being mode, can still refuse the mask, can still practice love as the active concern for the life and the growth of that which we love, starting with their own life, and their own growth. In my 1956 book *The Art of Loving* — still, decades later, one of the most widely read books ever written on the subject — I made a claim that unsettled readers precisely because it withheld the comfort they expected. Love, I insisted, is not a feeling you simply fall into, the way modern culture likes to imagine it, a stroke of luck that either happens to you or doesn't. It is an art, in the same sense that music or medicine or carpentry is an art: something that has to be learned, and that demands discipline, concentration, and patience long before it offers anything back. Most people, I have observed, spend their whole lives chasing the feeling of being loved, and almost none of the effort learning the far harder, far rarer capacity to love. That same discipline, I believe, is exactly what the being mode requires and what the having mode quietly trains us out of — because accumulating a possession takes far less of a person than growing a capacity does.

I have not tied this up into a tidy ending for you, because that has never been my way, and this reflection stops on the image of a civilization mistaking its machines for its maturity. But everything I have built across fifty years of clinical work and a shelf of books I hope outlive me says the hope was always there, underneath the diagnosis, waiting on the other side of the discomfort. I do not believe people are broken beyond repair. I believe they have been taught, systematically, to mistake a mask for a face — and that the first and hardest step is simply seeing the difference.

If what I have told you made you look differently at the mask you put on this morning, that is exactly the discomfort I have always tried to produce. Sit with it rather than rushing past it — that is where the actual work begins. If you would like me to go inside more of my own books this closely — *Escape from Freedom*, *The Art of Loving*, *The Sane Society*, or *To Have or to Be?* — subscribe, and tell me in the comments which one you want to hear next.
