## SCRIPT

There is a moment in every honest conversation with Erich Fromm where the room seems to go quiet. Not because he raises his voice — he never does — but because he says, plainly, the thing everyone around him has silently agreed never to say out loud. In this interview, recorded late in his life, he says it in the first minute: we live in a society of chronically unhappy people. Not occasionally unhappy. Not situationally unhappy. Chronically. As a permanent condition, dressed up every morning to look like something else.

Before we sit with his words, it is worth knowing who is speaking. Erich Fromm was born in Frankfurt am Main on March 23, 1900, into an Orthodox Jewish family, and by his own account spent much of his youth trying to understand why people — otherwise reasonable, otherwise decent people — could be swept into collective madness. He watched it happen twice: once as a teenager during the First World War, and again as a grown man in the rise of Nazism, which forced him to flee Germany in 1933. In between, he had trained as a psychoanalyst at the Berlin Institute of Psychoanalysis starting in 1926, following his own analysis — a detail that matters, because everything he says in this interview about "empirical findings" is not armchair philosophy. It is the residue of decades spent in a room with real, suffering people. He was associated with the Frankfurt Institute for Social Research — the group history remembers as the Frankfurt School — where psychoanalysis was turned, for the first time at that scale, into a tool for examining not just the individual mind, but the sickness of entire societies. That is the lens he brings to this interview. Keep it in mind, because it explains why his answers keep sliding from the personal to the political without ever feeling like a change of subject. For Fromm, there was no meaningful difference. The patient on the couch and the society outside the window were the same illness, viewed at two different distances.

That refusal to separate the psychological from the economic became the organizing idea of his entire body of work, and it has a name: social character. Fromm argued that every society, in order to function, needs its members to want to do what they have to do — to feel, from the inside, a genuine desire to work the jobs, buy the goods, and live the life that the economic system requires of them. A society doesn't survive by force alone; it survives by shaping character itself, from childhood onward, until the demands of the system feel like the natural preferences of the individual. That shaping process is what he called the social character of a given culture — not each person's private, unique personality, but the shared core of drives and values stamped into nearly everyone who grows up inside the same economic structure. It is a quiet, almost invisible kind of engineering. Nobody sits a child down and instructs them to prize productivity over rest, or possessions over relationships. The economy does that instruction for them, continuously, through what gets rewarded and what gets ignored. By the time Fromm is sitting for this interview, late in his life, he is no longer describing a theory. He is describing a diagnosis he has watched confirmed, patient after patient, for half a century.

So when the interviewer opens with that quote — "we live in a society of chronically unhappy people" — and asks how he arrived at something so bold, Fromm's answer is almost impatient. Listen again to what he actually says.

**Interviewer:** You once said: "We live in a society of chronically unhappy people." How did you arrive at such a bold statement?

**Erich Fromm:** To me, that statement isn't bold at all – quite the opposite. If one only opens their eyes, one can see it. Most people pretend – to themselves as well – that they are happy. Because if you're unhappy, you're considered – as one would say in English – a failure. So you must wear the mask of contentment, of happiness, otherwise you lose credit on the market. Then you're no longer a normal, capable person. But just look at people – it's plain to see what lies behind the mask: Restlessness, irritability, anger, depression, sleeplessness, unhappiness – what the French called "la malaise." Already at the beginning of the century people spoke of the "malaise du siècle" – what Freud called "the discontent in civilization." But it isn't discontent in civilization – it is discontent in bourgeois society, which turns man into a work animal. Everything that truly matters – the ability to love, to be there for oneself and for others, to think, not to be an instrument of the economy but its purpose – that is what makes people the way they are. I believe it's a general fiction people share, that modern man is happy. But this observation is not mine alone – you can find it in the work of many others. One only needs to open one's eyes and not be deceived by appearances.

Notice the phrase he reaches for almost casually: "you lose credit on the market." Fromm is not being poetic. He means it in the most literal, economic sense he can. Years earlier, in *Man for Himself*, he had already described what he called the marketing orientation — a way of relating to your own personality as if it were a product to be sold. Under the marketing orientation, a person doesn't ask "who am I, and what do I feel?" They ask "what does the market want me to be right now, so that I remain in demand?" Success is not measured by whether your inner life is coherent or whole. It is measured by whether you sell — whether you are liked, promoted, approved of, invited back. And the terrifying part of this, which Fromm spent his career trying to make visible, is that the mask stops feeling like a mask. It becomes the only self the person has left access to. He called the end state of this process automaton conformity: a person who has surrendered so completely to what is expected of them that they no longer experience the surrender as loss. They feel, if anything, relief — because an automaton is never anxious about failing to be itself. It has nothing left to fail at. That is the machinery running underneath the sentence "you must wear the mask of contentment." It is not vanity. It is a survival strategy in a system that prices unhappiness as a defect.

Automaton conformity was never, for Fromm, the only exit from that anxiety — only the quietest one. In *Escape from Freedom*, the 1941 book that first made his name, written in the shadow of the Nazism he had just fled, Fromm had laid out three ways a person can respond to the unbearable freedom and isolation of modern life, once the old certainties of tradition, community, and fixed social roles have dissolved. One path is authoritarianism — surrendering the self not to a faceless market this time, but to a strongman, a party, an ideology, fusing your identity with something that feels bigger and more certain than you are, so the terror of standing alone disappears. Another is destructiveness — turning outward, against the world, in an attempt to eliminate the very things that make you feel small and threatened. And the third, the one directly relevant to this interview, is automaton conformity — the quiet path, the one that doesn't march or rage, but simply dissolves the self into whatever the surrounding culture expects, until there is no longer a private self left to feel afraid. Fromm wrote that book to explain how an educated, cultured nation could hand itself over to Hitler. Decades later, in this interview, he is describing the same escape mechanism operating in peacetime democracies, without tanks or rallies — just a mask, worn every morning, because the alternative feels too exposed.

This is also where Fromm's famous distinction between happiness and pleasure quietly enters the interview, even though he never uses the word "pleasure" directly here. The mask of contentment produces something that looks like happiness from the outside — a person who smiles, performs, and is rewarded for it — but Fromm insisted throughout his work that this kind of manufactured cheerfulness is not the same thing as a life that is actually being lived well. Real well-being, for Fromm, could never be reduced to the momentary relief of having pleased someone else, or having sold well on the market that day. It required something closer to wholeness: a person's feelings, thoughts, and actions all pointing in the same direction, rather than the feelings buried under a performance designed for other people's approval. That gap — between looking content and being whole — is precisely the gap his patients kept falling into, and precisely the gap he is describing when he says people are "restless, irritable, angry, depressed, sleepless" underneath a face arranged to suggest otherwise.

The interviewer then presses him on where this insight actually comes from — not as an abstract social theory, but as something a therapist sees, up close, again and again.

**Interviewer:** Not be deceived by appearances, as you once said as a therapist – this comes from your experience in therapy, doesn't it?

**Erich Fromm:** Exactly. I've been analyzing since 1926 and supervised hundreds of cases by younger colleagues. These are empirical findings. People came as patients usually because of small symptoms, this or that. What I first had to help them realize was that they were deeply unhappy. That they were dissatisfied with life, that life seemed meaningless – and that from this came their various symptoms, attempts to compensate for their unhappiness.

This is a crucial hinge in the interview, easy to rush past. Fromm is drawing a straight line from Freud's individual unconscious to something Freud himself never fully mapped: what Fromm called the social unconscious — the vast portion of a person's experience that is repressed not because it is personally shameful, but because the surrounding society has no category for it, no language that would let it be spoken and heard. Freud's unconscious was built mostly out of forbidden individual drives, things a single person's own upbringing had taught them to bury. Fromm's addition was to ask: what if an entire society, through its language, its rewarded behaviors, and its unspoken assumptions, teaches millions of people at once to bury the same experiences? What if a whole culture, in effect, agrees not to notice something, and each individual inherits that blind spot along with everything else they're handed at birth? A patient arrives with insomnia, or a nagging anxiety, or an irritability they can't explain, and the presenting complaint is almost never the real subject. Underneath it, Fromm is saying, there is nearly always the same discovery waiting: this person has quietly concluded that their life is meaningless, and has never once been permitted to say so out loud, not even to themselves, because nothing in the culture around them gave that feeling a name or a place to go. The symptom is what leaks out when meaning has nowhere else to go. By the time he is speaking in this interview, he has spent close to fifty years watching that same pattern repeat, across patients, across countries, across decades — which is why he calls it empirical. He is not hypothesizing. He is reporting a result, observed so many times it has stopped being a theory and become, in his mind, simply a fact about modern life.

That fact leads directly into the interview's most provocative turn — the moment Fromm inverts the entire premise of what health and illness even mean.

**Interviewer:** Does that mean that the people we call "normal" are, from your point of view, actually ill?

**Erich Fromm:** Oh yes. The most normal are the sickest, and the sick are the healthiest. That may sound witty or exaggerated, but I mean it quite seriously – it's not a clever phrase. The person who is ill shows that certain human qualities in them are not yet so suppressed that they no longer conflict with cultural patterns. And that conflict – that friction – produces symptoms. A symptom, like pain, is only a sign that something is wrong. Happy is the one who has a symptom, as happy as the one who feels pain when something is missing. We know that if humans couldn't feel pain, they would be in great danger. But many people – the so-called "normal" ones – are so adapted, have so abandoned what is truly their own, are so alienated, so mechanical, so robotic, that they no longer even feel conflict. Their real feelings, their love, their hate – all that has been so repressed or atrophied that they resemble a state of chronic mild schizophrenia.

This is not a throwaway paradox. It is the explicit, load-bearing thesis of his 1955 book *The Sane Society*, where Fromm argues that a society itself can be sick, and that when it is, the people who adapt to it most completely are not the healthy ones — they are the most thoroughly infected. He gave this condition a name that has outlived the book itself: the pathology of normalcy. The idea is unsettling precisely because it removes the usual escape hatch. We are used to thinking of mental illness as a private failure, a deviation from a healthy baseline that everyone else occupies. Fromm turns that completely around. If the baseline itself — the culture, the economy, the accepted way of living — is what's diseased, then conforming perfectly to it is not evidence of health. It is evidence of the deepest possible infection, one so complete the person no longer feels the friction that would normally announce that something is wrong. That is what he means when he says the "normal" person has become "mechanical" and "robotic" enough to resemble, in his own startling phrase, "a state of chronic mild schizophrenia" — not a clinical diagnosis, but a description of a self so divided from its own real feelings that it has stopped noticing the split. Meanwhile the patient with a visible symptom — the insomnia, the anxiety, the depression — is, paradoxically, still fighting. Some part of them still refuses to fully surrender to a system Fromm believed was built to devour exactly what is most human in a person. The symptom is the last flicker of resistance. That is why, in his telling, it is almost a mark of health.

This is also the point in Fromm's thinking where psychology stops being a private matter at all and becomes, unavoidably, a political one. If entire populations can be rendered "sick" by the structure of the society they live in, then treating the individual patient — helping one person adjust, calm down, cope better — can only ever be a partial answer. A therapist working from that premise cannot, in good conscience, treat successful adjustment to a sick society as the goal of therapy, because perfect adjustment is exactly the condition Fromm has just finished describing as the deepest form of the illness. This is part of why Fromm never comfortably fit the label of a purely clinical psychoanalyst. He kept insisting that the couch and the culture were the same case file, and that no diagnosis of the individual was complete without a diagnosis of the society standing behind them.

Having built that foundation, the interviewer asks him to name the machine actually producing all of this — and here Fromm stops speaking as a therapist and starts speaking as the social critic who had, by then, spent a lifetime studying economic systems as carefully as he studied the psyche.

**Interviewer:** Do you see the causes for this in our society?

**Erich Fromm:** The causes seem quite obvious to me. Our society is built on the principle that the goal of life is ever greater production – as compensation and as necessity – and ever greater consumption. Economic and technological progress – that is what we live for, not the human being. What benefits man matters little. Even what harms him hardly matters. Much of it is well known: advertisements promote things that are deadly and harmful. For example – as was recently discussed – milk shipped to Africa that persuades women not to nurse their children, leading to many infant deaths. But that doesn't matter – when profit is at stake, there's no conscience about harming people. And if you want an even starker example, just think of profits from the arms industry, the sale of weapons across the world, even atomic energy. What happens to people in the process is irrelevant. That began only in the industrial age. In the Middle Ages it wasn't so – nor in most cultures. Back then, culture served humanity, and the economy was a means, not an end. Freud himself offered one of the sharpest critiques of bourgeois society, without realizing it. Bourgeois society is a neurotic one – ruled by the principle of having: of accumulating, hoarding, clinging, of greed in the end.

Notice, too, the exact words Fromm chooses here — "accumulating, hoarding, clinging." Those are not casual synonyms he reached for in the moment. Decades earlier, in *Man for Himself*, he had already mapped out a typology of what he called character orientations, and one of them he named, precisely, the hoarding orientation: a way of relating to the world built on holding on — to money, to possessions, to feelings, even to other people — for fear that what is not held onto will be lost forever. He set that against what he called the productive orientation, the capacity to relate to the world by creating, loving, and reasoning rather than by accumulating. When Fromm tells this interviewer that bourgeois society is "ruled by the principle of having," he is not coining a new idea on the spot. He is reaching for technical vocabulary he had already spent thirty years refining, and applying it, in real time, to the whole civilization sitting outside the studio.

That closing line — "the principle of having" — is the seed of what would become, in 1976, the book many consider Fromm's final major statement: *To Have or to Be?* His argument there is deceptively simple. There are two fundamentally different ways a person can relate to existence. In the having mode, your sense of self is built out of what you possess — property, status, credentials, relationships treated as trophies, even ideas hoarded rather than lived. The trouble with building an identity this way, Fromm warns, is that it is permanently unstable, because everything you have can be lost. He put the danger in a single unforgettable question: if I am what I have, and if I lose what I have, who then am I? In the being mode, by contrast, your sense of self comes from what you actively do and experience — loving, creating, thinking, growing — activity that cannot be confiscated, because it isn't a possession in the first place. Fromm believed industrial capitalism had organized an entire civilization almost exclusively around the having mode, and had gone further still, manufacturing what he called homo consumens — consuming man — a human being whose identity is measured, and constantly re-measured, by what they acquire and use up. That is the economic machine underneath the interview's small, devastating example of infant formula marketed to mothers in Africa: a system, Fromm is arguing, that will manufacture demand and sell a product even when the product costs infant lives, because within the having mode, profit is the only variable the system is actually designed to protect. Not because the individuals running it are monsters — Fromm is careful never to say that — but because the logic of the system doesn't include a column for human cost. It was never built to.

That leads to the interview's final turn, where the interviewer asks Fromm to follow his own argument to its most uncomfortable conclusion.

**Interviewer:** Then, following that logic, Mr. Fromm, would the industrial society actually be the underdeveloped one?

**Erich Fromm:** Humanly speaking, it certainly is the underdeveloped society. If I look at a tribal society in Africa, or a simple peasant community where such life still exists – they are far more developed, humanly, than we are. Modern man believes that the more machines one needs, the more advanced one is. But in truth, the fewer machines one needs, the more developed one is. And so he looks down upon those who do not build, use, or depend on machines.

It is worth sitting with how radical this reversal actually is, because it is easy to nod along and miss what Fromm is doing to the entire vocabulary of "progress." He is not romanticizing poverty, and he is not naive about hardship. What he is rejecting is the assumption — so deeply built into modern life that it rarely gets questioned at all — that technological complexity and human development are the same measurement. Elsewhere in his work he put the stakes of that confusion in blunt terms: the danger of the past was that men became slaves; the danger of the future, he warned, is that men may become robots. A robot, in his sense, is not science fiction. It is a fully adapted, fully "normal," fully productive member of an industrial society who has quietly stopped being able to love, to feel, to want anything that isn't first approved by the market. By that measurement — not output, not GDP, not machines owned — a community that has kept its capacity to love, to grieve openly, to sit with another person without a screen or a schedule between them, is, in Fromm's phrase, more developed. Not more comfortable. Not wealthier. More developed, as a human being.

Fromm gave this tension its fullest and darkest treatment a few years before this interview, in his 1973 book *The Anatomy of Human Destructiveness*, where he introduced a pair of concepts he considered the deepest fork in human character: biophilia and necrophilia. Biophilia, as he defined it, is the passionate love of life and of everything alive — a person, a plant, an idea, a social group — the drive to help things grow rather than to control or freeze them. Necrophilia, in his specific psychological sense, has nothing to do with the clinical meaning of the word. He used it to describe a passionate attraction to everything dead, mechanical, and fixed — an orientation that would rather dominate and control the living than tolerate its unpredictability, that prefers the certainty of machines to the mess of people. Fromm's warning, in that book and in the years after it, was that a civilization built entirely around production, machinery, and control was quietly cultivating necrophilia at a civilizational scale — teaching its members, generation after generation, to feel more at ease with the mechanical and the controllable than with the living and the unpredictable. That is the deeper current underneath his claim, in this interview, that a society measuring its worth in machines has the measurement backward. It isn't only inefficient by his standard. It is training its people, slowly and invisibly, to love the wrong things.

That is where this particular exchange ends, but it is worth naming plainly what Fromm has actually built across these few minutes, because it is easy to experience it as five separate answers rather than one argument. He starts with a mask — the performed happiness everyone agrees to wear. He traces that mask back to decades of clinical evidence that behind it sits real, undiagnosed despair. He inverts the entire concept of normal and sick, so that adaptation itself becomes suspect. He locates the engine of all of it in an economic system organized around having rather than being. And he ends by asking us to consider that the society proudest of its own advancement might, by the only measurement he considered worth taking — how fully alive its people actually are — be the most underdeveloped one on earth.

None of this was meant, by Fromm, as despair for its own sake. He spent his whole life insisting the opposite was possible — that a person could still choose the being mode, could still refuse the mask, could still, in his own words, practice love "as the active concern for the life and the growth of that which we love," starting with their own life, and their own growth. In his 1956 book *The Art of Loving* — still, decades later, one of the most widely read books ever written on the subject — Fromm made a claim that unsettled readers precisely because it withheld the comfort they expected. Love, he insisted, is not a feeling you simply fall into, the way modern culture likes to imagine it, a stroke of luck that either happens to you or doesn't. It is an art, in the same sense that music or medicine or carpentry is an art: something that has to be learned, and that demands discipline, concentration, and patience long before it offers anything back. Most people, he argued, spend their whole lives chasing the feeling of being loved, and almost none of the effort learning the far harder, far rarer capacity to love. That same discipline, he believed, is exactly what the being mode requires and what the having mode quietly trains us out of — because accumulating a possession takes far less of a person than growing a capacity does.

The interview itself does not end on that hopeful note explicitly. Fromm was never in the business of tidy endings, and this conversation stops mid-argument, on the image of a civilization mistaking its machines for its maturity. But everything he built across fifty years of clinical work and a shelf of books that outlived him says the hope was always there, underneath the diagnosis, waiting on the other side of the discomfort. He did not believe people were broken beyond repair. He believed they had been taught, systematically, to mistake a mask for a face — and that the first and hardest step was simply seeing the difference.

If this conversation made you look differently at the mask you put on this morning, that is exactly the discomfort Fromm was trying to produce. Sit with it rather than rushing past it — that is where he believed the actual work begins.
